# 2. The mechanical drive train

The mechanical drive train of a bicycle has three main components:

* The front crank set, with one or more chainrings.
* The rear cassette, with one or more cogs (sprockets).
* The chain used to connect the selected chain ring and cog.
  
The operation of the drive train is ruled by the laws of classical physics, mainly:

* Circular Motion.
* Conservation of Energy.
  
When the rider pushes down on the pedal with a force $F_{pedal}$, it generates a torque:

$$\tau_{crank} = F_{pedal} \times r_{crank}$$

where $$r_{crank}$$ is the radius of the crank arm.

The front chainring converts this torque into a linear tension force $T$ along the chain:

$$T = \frac{\tau_{crank}}{r_{chainring}}$$

where $$r_{chainring}$$ is the radius of the chainring.

This tension force pulls on the teeth of the rear cog, generating a torque at the rear hub. Assuming a lossless drivetrain, the tension force at the rear cog is the same as the tension force generated by the chainring, hence the rear torque is: 

$$\tau_{rear} = T \times r_{cog} = \tau_{crank} \times \left(\frac{r_{cog}}{r_{chainring}}\right)$$

where $$r_{cog}$$ is the radius of the cog.

If we define $$\frac{r_{chainring}}{r_{cog}}$$ as the Gear Ratio $G_{r}$, the above equation can be rewritten as follows:

$$\tau_{crank} = \tau_{rear} \times G_{r}$$

In other words, the drivetrain acts as a simple _torque transformer_.

And because the geometry of the teeth on the chainring and cog is the same, the gear ratio can be expressed simply in terms of their respective number of teeth $N$:

$$G_{r} = \frac{N_{chainring}}{N_{cog}}$$

Thus the value of $G_{r}$ is smallest when using the smallest chainring and the largest cog, the so called "granny gear", which results in the lowest torque on the crank, and the easiest pedaling effort.
